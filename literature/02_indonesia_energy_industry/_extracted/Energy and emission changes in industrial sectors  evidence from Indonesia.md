## ABSTRACT

Indonesia, like many countries around the world, has embedded the low-carbon paradigm into its development plan by reducing energy and carbon intensity. To design the appropriate policy, the government needs information on which sectors have the highest energy intensity and what are the main drivers of energy emissions. We investigate the  lifecycle  energy  and  emission  intensity  of  industrial  sectors  by  using  the  Hybrid Energy Input-Output (HEIO) table and Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) technique.  Applying  this  to  the  Indonesian  economy  from  2010  to  2016,  we  find  that  nonmetallic  minerals  and  metal  products  have  the  highest  total  energy  and  emissions intensity. Upon examining the impact of indirect energy use and energy consumption patterns, we discover the hidden energy- and emission-intensive industries that would otherwise  be  deemed  insignificant  through  the  common  lens  of  production-based energy consumption. The SDA analysis concludes that economic development aspects have  become  the  primary  driver  of  increased  energy  consumption  and  emission, whereas energy conversion and production technology only affect the changes marginally.  Evidence  with  such  granularity  would  assist  the  policy  makers  in  Indonesia  and other countries with similar economic structures in developing better-targeted energy policies to achieve climate-compatible development.

## 1. Introduction

Global warming is a grand challenge of our time: by 2050, all countries and entities must reduce emissions and neutralize the remaining unabated emissions, mainly from the energy sector as the most significant contributor, to be on track with a best-case 1.5° scenario (IPCC 2022). However, the current policies would bring us far above the allowable 2° limit, and the pledges and targets would take us only marginally above it (Ritchie, Roser and Rosado 2023). Even with this modest warming, catastrophic implications become a near certainty  (Eckstein,  Kunzel,  and  Schafer  2021,  Kemp  et  al.  2022,  Vargas  Zeppetello,  Raftery  and  Battisti 2022). Therefore, better-targeted cross-sectoral climate policies on industries are needed to avoid climate disasters.

Many countries have embedded a low-carbon paradigm into their development plan by reducing energy and carbon intensity. Indonesia, for example, envisaged that in 2030, GHG emissions would be reduced by 31% unilaterally  or  43%,  conditional  on  international  support  (Kementerian  PPN/  Bappenas,  2019).  In 2016, the energy sector in Indonesia contributed 40% of the total GHG emissions (OECD 2022). Of this figure, more than 80% were committed by the industrial sector to generate heat or drive electrical machinery (BPS 2021a). With hundreds of industrial sectors and complex interlinkages between them, which particular sectors does Indonesia need to focus on?

This  paper  attempts  to  answer  two  main  research  questions.  First,  which  industrial  sectors  have  the highest  lifecycle  emission  and  energy  intensity?  Examining  the  intensity  from  the  perspective  of  final demand could unearth details on lifecycle energy consumption and emissions that are often overlooked. The  lifecycle  approach  is  essential  to  reveal  the  actual  energy-  and  emission-intensive  sectors,  thus

Second, what are the main drivers of sectoral energy and energy-related emission changes of three main gases, CO2, CH4, and NO2? Answering these research questions requires techniques linking complex economic networks with energy and emissions. The Hybrid Energy IO (HEIO) is well suited to address the requirement as it can trace the economy's direct and indirect energy or emission impacts. Moreover, a Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA)  is  conducted  to  decompose  the  energy  and  emission  changes  into  technological  and  economic development effects, thus providing an integrated perspective on the problems.

Numerous studies have analyzed the lifecycle environmental impacts of economic activities in Indonesia, mainly employing the Extended Environmental Input-Output (EEIO). The versatility of EEIO in conducting lifecycle analysis (LCA) is reflected by the various environmental aspects covered in the studies. Some topics focused on the non-energy impacts, such as water pollution (Resosudarmo 2003), forest degradation and land use change (Aryapratama and Pauliuk 2022, Pirmana et al. 2021), and water consumption (Mohan et  al.  2021).  Others  evaluated  energy  and  energy-related  emissions  but  attempted  to  highlight  different perspectives. For instance, Putranti and Imansyah (2017) estimated the income elasticity of emissions from the manufacturing sectors. In addition, other researchers studied household behaviors on emissions (Irfany and  Klasen  2017,  Shrestha  and  Marpaung  1998),  lifecycle  energy,  and  emissions  in  electric  generation systems (Widiyanto, Kato and Maruyama 2003) or buildings (Surahman 2014).

In  contrast,  only  a  few  studies  have  attempted  to  discover  the  drivers  of  energy  and  emissions  using Structural Decomposition Analysis (SDA) in Indonesia. Hastuti et al. (2021) implemented SDA on EEIO to analyze energy-related CO2 emissions between 1990-1995 and 2010-2015. They converted the monetary IO  table  using  sectoral  energy  intensity  and  conducted  mathematical  manipulations  to  decompose  the effects of emission change in 48 sectors. Another example is Marpaung and Shresta (2019), who decomposed the tax energy effect on CO2 reduction using the EEIO table as the primary tool. However, the EEIObased  LCA  is  prone  to  bias  from  the  proportionality  assumption  because  the  physical  unit  flow,  e.g., emissions  or  energy,  is  represented  by  pre-calculated  units  per  economic  activity,  thus  constraining  the accuracy and depth of the analysis (Lenzen 2002). Therefore, there is a gap in the Indonesian literature for an economy-wide lifecycle study that can trace the energy and emissions from extraction to final consumers with recent IO data.

This paper contributes to the literature in several ways. First, it is the first to utilize HEIO in Indonesia, which  explicitly  maintains  the  energy  conservation  principle  and  single  emission  counting.  As  such,  the analysis could be traced back to the associated energy sources. Second, it employs Indonesia's synchronized 83sector IO Tables (the most disaggregated tables thus far for energy study on Indonesia) and energy consumption data by sectors to avoid the loss of information during the analysis. Consequently, the decomposition can be made into greater detail of sectors to inform policymakers in Indonesia and other developing countries, which rely on resource-based and medium-low technology manufacturing, specifically for the policies that link energy and industrial/sectoral policies. Third, the paper employs the most recent official data, which is vital to evaluate past policies and inform what worked/did not work for future policy making.

## 2. Literature review

Hybrid Energy IO (HEIO) is a mixed-unit IO that replaces the energy transaction with physical units and leaves the remaining in monetary units (Figure 1). Built on the work of Bullard and Herendeen (1975), HEIO has been increasingly used to analyze lifecycle or embodied energy and emissions in the economy (S. D. Casler and Blair 1997, S. Casler and Wilbur 1984, Lindner and Guan 2014, Treloar 1997) by tracking energy use from the ground to the final demand (Hannon 2010). Some researchers also expanded the use of HEIO to account for the embodied energy or emissions in international trade (Machado, Schaeffer, and Worrell 2001) or replaced energy with other material flows, such as minerals (Giljum and Hubacek 2004, Hawkins et al. 2007).

HEIO inherently conforms  to  important  energy  conservation  principles,  which  posit  that  the  energy output of a conversion process should be equal to energy input plus conversion losses (Miller and Blair 2009). Figure 2 illustrates the principle, which allows the traceback to primary sources and delineates the direct  and  indirect  energy  requirements.  Ueda  (2022)  modifies  the  models  to  comply  with  the  single emission counting on top of conversion principles and extends the methods introduced by Kagawa and Inamura (2001) to disaggregate energy and non-energy changes to energy consumption.

HEIO complements Environmentally Extended Input Output (EEIO) analysis in addressing the lifecycle emissions and energy in the economy (Hoekstra and van den Bergh 2002, Hoekstra and van den Bergh 2006, Suh et al. 2004). EEIO is a monetary IO model coupled with a corresponding vector to reflect the physical unit per output, including energy. Relative to EEIO, HEIO is superior in some ways:

- (1) HEIO is not prone to different commodity prices faced by each sector (Hoekstra and van den Bergh 2002, Kagawa and Inamura 2001). For instance, diesel consumption for industries and household consumers might differ due to implicit subsidies or other price controls for households. Conversely, EEIO  encounters  proportionality  assumptions,  which  assume  the  price  is  homogeneous  among sectors (Hawkins et al. 2007, Williams, Weber and Hawkins 2009).
- (2) HEIO allows  explicit  energy  descriptions  outside  the  economy,  such  as  solar  power.  In  contrast, EEIO models are limited to attributing energy/emissions to those explicitly recorded in the economy, such as fossil fuels.
- (3) HEIO preserves the cascaded flow from primary to secondary energy and finally to non-energy sectors, thus making all analysis, energy, or emission attributable to the energy sources. On the contrary, the EEIO lumps the energy/emission coefficient into the total energy consumed or emission per output unit and loses its granularity (Guevara, Rodrigues, and Domingos 2015, Ueda 2022).

## Nevertheless, HEIO has some drawbacks:

- (1) HEIO  involves  more  complex  data  requirements.  Furthermore,  it  coalesces  the  energy  data  and monetary IO, which are already data-intensive and often have different sectoral divisions.
- (2) HEIO does not allow further decomposition of demand effects into final demand mix, allocation, and others,  as  some  studies  exemplify  (Arto  and  Dietzenbacher  2014,  Cansino,  Román,  and  Ordóñez 2016). It is constrained only to demand scale effects as its mix-unit induces arbitrary results for other decompositions (Dietzenbacher 2006).

Structural decomposition analysis (SDA) has been employed to complement input-output (IO) analysis in uncovering the driving factors behind changes over time (Ang 2004, Dietzenbacher and Los 1998, Su and Ang 2012). Its application is increasingly important in the context of energy and emissions (Lenzen 2016). Since 2019, studies focusing on China have dominated the literature (R. Li et al. 2023; Y. Li, Lin, and Su 2025; Lin et al. 2022; Yan and Su 2020), primarily utilizing the polar decomposition method to delineate the drivers of energy consumption, emission, and their intensities in relation to updates of the Five-Year Plan (FYP). Polar decomposition can be approximated by the simple average of pair decomposition (i.e., twopolar decomposition), a methodology frequently used in academic studies (Arto and Dietzenbacher 2014), despite the deviation from the full decomposition (Su and Ang 2012). 1 Furthermore, other SDA studies have concentrated on specific countries utilizing their own single-region IO data for a production-based analysis (Cansino, Román, and Ordóñez 2016, Y. Li, Lin, and Su 2025, Lim, Yoo, and Kwak 2009, Radwan et al. 2022, Su,  Ang and Li 2017, Zhu, Su and Li 2018) or multi-regional IO for a consumption-based analysis that accounts for embedded emissions in trade (Hoekstra, Michel, and Suh 2016, Wang et al. 2024, Wang, Ang and Su 2017).

The main difference between our study and that of previous ones such as Su et al. (2017) is how E d ,  the coefficient  or intensity  factor ,  is  being  derived.  Su  et  al.  (2017)  and  other  papers  that  employ Environmentally Extended IO (EEIO) such as Hoekstra and van den Bergh (2002) and Hoekstra and van den Bergh 2006) and Suh et al. For the rest of the model (i.e., structural decomposition analysis - see below) we utilize a two-polar decomposition analysis based on the equations of interest (Equation 7 for energy and Equation 10 for emission).

## 4. Results and discussion

Energy and emission intensities presented in this paper are derived from hybrid input-output (IO) tables, enabling  the  tracing  of  both  direct  and  indirect  energy  and  emission  impacts.  The  methodology  for calculating  these  intensities  differs  from  that  of  Extended  Environmental  IO  (EEIO)  models,  which  are typically computed externally to the model. This approach offers the advantage of tracking energy back to its  primary  sources,  thus  providing  the  flexibility  to  analyze  from  both  'primary  form'  and  'secondary form' perspectives. This distinction is crucial for policymakers, as those consuming energy in its secondary form do not have control over the associated emissions - commonly referred to as Scope 2 emissions in corporate sustainability regulations, in contrast to Scope 1 emissions, which are directly controlled by the corporation.  Consequently,  the  required  policies  will  differ;  those  who  primarily  consume  energy  in  its primary form can directly manage their emissions, which fall under their Scope 1 responsibilities.

## 4.1. Energy intensity

As depicted in Table 4, the nonmetallic mineral products cluster stands out as the cluster with the highest primary energy intensity in the economy. In 2016, this cluster consumed 3.84 GJ per million IDR (46.60 GJ per thousand USD) of final demand, dropping by 17% from 2010. This drop could be primarily attributed to lower coal and natural gas consumption, especially in the cement industry. Between 2010 and 2016, the cement industry reduced the coal intensity by 14% to 5.781 GJ (0.23 tonnes) and halved the gas intensity to 1.061  GJ  (27.3 m 3 )  per  million  IDR  final  demand.  As  there  were  no  apparent  increases  in  other  energy products, these reductions indicated the overall energy efficiency of the cluster.

Indirect energy intensity becomes critical in the extractive, downstream manufacturing, and construction clusters. Decarbonization  for  clusters  with  high-embodied  energy  and  their  ensuing  emissions  could  be  done through material circularity - a straightforward but often overlooked approach. Unlike technology interventions that require a high upfront cost, material circularity can be implemented at a more competitive price  by  optimizing  the  material  uses  and  recycling  end-of-life  materials.  Material  Economics  (2018) estimated that the emissions related to steel materials could be reduced by 37% by mid-century through recycling  and  material  optimization. Clusters  with  more  secondary  energy  consumption  can  be  decarbonized  with  electrification  and  fuel switching. It is particularly suitable for light-duty manufacturing, transportation, construction, and services requiring less technical complexity. Transport-based sectors can pursue the avenue of e-mobility or fuel switch to low-carbon fuels, such as biodiesel. Similarly, clean fuels and electrification solutions, such as heat pumps, could be solutions for sectors that need low-carbon heat in their production processes. This suite of technologies is now available at competitive prices, thanks to rapid advancement in technology innovation and deployment. With appropriate market-based policy instruments, the transition can entail.

Clusters with high primary energy consumption typically require more complex technological intervention, complicating their decarbonization path. For instance, primary metals and cement industries operate at high temperatures to enable chemical reactions in their manufacturing processes. Thereby, the general electrification solution would not be sufficient. Another alternative, hydrogen, could be a potential solution if generated from renewable sources or fossil fuels with carbon capture. However, it comes with hefty prices, which  could  add  substantial  costs  to  the  commodity's  wholesale  prices.  Therefore,  pull  mechanism environmental regulations and carbon pricing could be developed to enable the least-cost decarbonization for these clusters. These policies aim to create demand for low-carbon commodities and bolster recycling efforts to reduce carbon-intensive production. Moreover, implementing these domestic policies will help the  country  navigate  trade-related  environmental  policy,  such  as  the  border  carbon  adjustment  (BCA), which will utilize trade mechanisms to advance environmental objectives. BCA and other similar policies, typically targeting industries with high primary energy consumption, have shown that climate change has emerged  as  the  second-strongest  motivation  behind  recent  policy  interventions  (Evenett  et  al.  2024). Consequently, Indonesia and other countries with similar economic structures are at the juncture where adopting these global trends will reshape the nation's economic landscape.

## 4.2. Emission intensity

Total  emission  intensity  (tCO2e  per  IDR  million  of  final  demand)  provides  insight  into  total  GHG emissions from direct fossil fuel combustion and non-energy consumption that embody the emissions in their production stage. The total emissions intensity covers primary gases from energy combustion, such as CO2, CH4, and N2O, which are converted to CO2 equivalent based on their global warming potential. Figure 5 displays the total emission intensity of 14 non-energy clusters from 2010 to 2016.

In general, a cluster with high energy intensity positively correlates with high total emission intensity, although this does not hold in some cases - for instance, direct biomass combustion. According to IPCC GHG Inventories Guidelines (IPCC 2019), biomass and biomass-based combustion are outside the scope of energy sector inventories. Instead, the emissions are accounted for in the AFOLU sector. This fact might have  an  important  implication  for  the  analysis  that  links  economic  activities,  energy  combustion,  and emissions.  One  should  be  careful  not  to  misconstrue  relatively  low  emissions  from  clusters  with  high biomass combustion, such as pulp, paper, and wood products. The emission release might happen elsewhere due to deforestation and land use changes.

From  2010  to  2016,  the  most  carbon-intensive  cluster  in  the  Indonesian  economy  was  nonmetallic mineral products. In 2010, this cluster emitted 0.36 tCO2e per million IDR (4.82 tCO2e per thousand USD) of the final demand it served. Fortunately, the intensity was improved to 0.31 tCO2e per million IDR (4.15 tCO2e  per  thousand  USD)  of  the  final  demand  because  of  energy  efficiency  improvement  in  coal  and natural gas intake. Another cluster that underwent emission intensity improvement was transportation. Air, road, and railway transport industries optimized their refined oil uptake, reducing emissions to serve the same one million. Although minor inefficiency occurred in water transport, the cluster weighted average was enhanced from 0.053 tCO2e (0.71 tCOe in thousand USD) in 2010 to 0.040 (0.54 tCOe in thousand USD) in 2016; all values were standardized to one million constant IDR final demand. Finally, the service cluster inconsiderably improved its emission intensity by 3.3 kgCO2e per million IDR (44.19 kgCO2e per thousand dollar) of final demand, driven by direct and structural efficiency of secondary energy products.

All remaining clusters experienced heightened emission intensity relative to the 2010 value, which could be attributable to three things. First, non-energy clusters augmented their fossil fuel use, such as coal and natural  gas,  to  generate  industrial  heat.  The  unabated  emission  from  the  combustion  of  these  fuels contributes  to  higher  total  emission  intensity.  Second,  these  clusters  consumed  more  carbon-intensive electricity. As electrification became prevalent among industries to drive electrical machinery or generate heat,  the  consumption  of  electricity  generated  from  fossil  fuel  power  plants  increased  the  emission intensities.  Third,  less  efficient  technology  also  contributed  to  more  emissions  in  the  production  stages. With  inefficient  technology,  production  might  employ  more  goods  with  high  embodied  emissions  and cause higher emission intensity for a cluster.

Emissions  from  natural  gas  show  other  noteworthy  dynamics.  From  2010  to  2016,  the  net  emission change from the natural gas was only -1.93% of the total emission change. This minor net change is caused by the drivers that canceled each other. The roaring domestic demand and 'worsening' structure led to an additional 36.4 million tCO2. This addition was then offset by the improvement in direct carbon intensity, mainly from the natural extraction process and LNG plant, and export reduction. These two effects made a final cut in emissions by 38.5 million tCO2.

Diesel oil  and  motor gasoline caused additional emissions of 29.8 million tCO2, primarily driven by an adverse change in carbon intensity and increased domestic demand. ## 5. Conclusions and policy implications

We have investigated the energy and intensity of industrial sectors using the case of Indonesia. We analyzed 83 energy and non-energy sectors in Indonesia and estimated their energy and emission intensities using the HEIO Table. Further, we conducted the SDA analysis to investigate the main drivers of energy and emission changes  between  2010  and  2016.  The  study  adds  value  to  the  IO  analysis  of  Indonesia's  economy  by enabling  the  attribution  of  energy  consumption  and  emissions  to  energy  sources  in  their  primary  and secondary forms. In doing so, the analysis could be made more detailed by examining the consumption patterns and revealing the portion of indirect energy and emission in a sector. Moreover, the study provides

a  benchmark  for  other  developing  economies  with  similar  economic  structures  to  identify  energy-  and emission-intensive industries, including those that indirectly use energy through production input and to navigate the emerging trade-related environmental policies adopted by advanced economies.

The dominant energy and emission intensity can be attributed to coal and natural gas. The nonmetallic minerals  and  metal  and  metal  products  clusters  are  the  largest  consumers  of  these  energy  products. Consequently,  clusters  that  consume  the  intermediate  inputs  from  nonmetallic  mineral  clusters,  e.g., cement,  or  metal  and  metal  products,  e.g.,  steel,  would  have  a  high  portion  of  indirect  energy.  Metalbased  manufacturing  and  construction  clusters  were  among  the  highest  in  the  indirect  energy  portion, ranging from 59% to 92% of their total energy intensity in 2016. In addition, some clusters consume more energy in their primary form, whereas others have more secondary energy intake. The distinct consumption patterns and other comprehensive findings add depth to policymaking as they allow bespoke approaches with more articulated sectoral targets.

Furthermore,  economic  developments  -  both  domestic  and  net  export  demand  -  became  the  most potent driver and eclipsed the technology's role in energy consumption and emission changes between 2010 and 2016. If anything, the technological effects, direct and indirect intensity, tend to indicate shifts from one energy carrier to others rather than energy efficiency or emission reduction. From a sectoral perspective, electricity, construction, transportation, and service clusters contribute to increased emissions through solid domestic demand. Surprisingly, the service cluster plays a significant role in emission changes despite its low energy and emission intensity relative to others. The net export effects are prominent in two clusters. The transport equipment cluster contributes negatively to the emission as they serve more robust export demand, whereas metals and metal products slash their net export and reduce their emissions.

These  results  bring  some  policy  implications.  The  minor  role  of  technological  effects  in  the  energy/ emission  change  indicates  that  the  energy  policy  did  not  produce  the  desired  change  in  the  period.  If Indonesia aims to reduce energy consumption and emissions while being better off economically, improvement in policy targeting is imperative. The policies should be able to target specific sectors and improve their direct as well as indirect technological elements accordingly.

There are two main avenues to improve the direct energy and emission intensity. First, a top-down policy to decarbonize the electricity systems and revamp the low carbon fuel production capacity could resolve the issues of high consumption and emission from secondary energy products. These policies involve technologies  with  high  market  readiness  levels;  however,  their  successful  deployment  depends  on  government commitment. Between 2010 and 2016, the Indonesian government issued several policies that gainsay their commitment toward a cleaner future. In the power sector, the government launched the Fast-track Program (FTP-2) but it was too heavy on coal technologies with little room for renewables (Hamdi and Adhiguna 2021). A feed-in tariff (FIT) for renewables was short-lived and unable to produce the desired outcome (Yuliani  2017).  In  2015,  the  government  revised  a  regulation  to  revamp  biofuel  blending  consumption across  industries,  although  unsustainable  palm  oil  plantation  practices  constrained  the  efforts  (Kharina, Malins, and Searle 2016). In hindsight, the government should have pursued policies to take advantage of the  global  decline  in  renewable  costs.  Moreover,  bioenergy  blending  strategies  should  emphasize  the importance of sustainability and alternative biomass with little to no ramifications to deforestation.

Second, an array of policies is needed to decarbonize clusters with high energy consumption in their primary forms, i.e., coal and natural gas. First and foremost, the policies should be able to mobilize finance and investment for cost-competitive technology innovation and deployment. In this regard, the government could  set  the  decarbonization  roadmap  and  earmark  funding  for  strategic  technologies,  such  as  green hydrogen and carbon capture and storage (IEA 2021b). For example, the InvestEU Fund in the European Union provides de-risking facilities  to  enable  more  than  Euro  372  billion  in  investment  for  low-carbon technologies (IEA 2022) on top of ongoing EU ETS that minimizes the abatement cost across industries. Moreover, the pull mechanism policies would create a niche market for low-carbon commodities (EU), thus providing industries with market certainty. Some countries pursue such initiatives globally, among other things, within the Clean Energy Ministerial Industrial Deep Decarbonization Initiatives (CEM IDDI), which attempt to leverage the procurement commitment for clean concrete and steel (Clean Energy Ministerial 2022).

Improvement  in  indirect  energy  and  emission  intensity  involves  the  policies  to  establish  material circularity for carbon- and energy-intensive materials. Material circularity is a vital mitigation lever that

<!-- image -->

reduces material use and improves the recycling rate to avoid the conventional production process in the first place. By targeting construction clusters, the Indonesian government could replicate France's approach in prescribing limits for new building lifecycle emissions (IEA (2021a). Additionally, product design that supports better recyclability and material efficiency can be regulated through design instruction. In 2022, the EU revised their Eco-design directives to include energy-related products, establishing frameworks to improve its circularity and environmental sustainability (European Commission 2022).

Some improvements could be made for future work. First, this study captures the energy policies and their  dynamics between 2010 and 2016; therefore, it does not capture the state-of-the-art development in  energy  sectors,  such  as  the  cost-competitiveness  of  renewable  energy  technologies  and  recent countries'  commitment  to  net  zero  by  mid-century.  These  updates  can  be  internalized  in  a  future study with forthcoming IO data. Second, 64 non-energy sector data aggregation has reduced the details initially offered in Indonesia's monetary IO data. It is an excellent opportunity to enhance the analysis when  energy  with  better  sectoral  granularity  becomes  available.  Similarly,  multi-regional  IO  data  can enrich the study with differentiated technologies embedded in imported goods. Finally, future analysis should  break  down  other  renewable  energy  in  more  detail.  The  newest  National  Electricity  Supply Business Plan (Ministry of Energy and Mineral Resources 2021) plans more renewable energy integrations;  therefore,  wind  and  solar  power  will  soon  become  the  centerpiece  of  Indonesia's  energy  system, indicating  a  better  and  cleaner  future.

Notwithsatnding  the  caveats,  the  evidence  presented  in  the  paper  would  assist  the  policy  makers  in Indonesia  and  in  other  countries  with  similar  economic  structures  in  developing  better-targeted  energy policies to achieve climate-compatible development.
