## ABSTRACT

This  paper  reports  an  examination  by  means  of  the  Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) to describe the decoupling indicators of CO2 emissions into key factors of Indonesia's manufacturing industry, namely, economic activity, industrial structure, energy intensity and energy structure in the period 2010-2014. The findings show that  the  impact  of  energy  efficiency  development  on  decoupling CO2  emissions in industrial sub-sector, the intensity of technology, and firm size are varied. Energy structures and industrial structures have little impact on decoupling CO2  emissions. Comparative analysis of industrial sub-sectors and other characteristics reveals that the  growth of industrial economic activity determines the decoupling status of CO2  emissions from industrial growth in Indonesia.

## Introduction

Climate change is a global problem [1-4]. It results from a significant increase in greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions caused by fossil fuel energy consumption and land-use changes [1,5]. The biggest contributor to GHG emissions is CO2 gas emissions, accounting for 60% of  total  GHGs  [1,4].  Indonesia  needs  to  make  effective  changes  both  because  of  its vulnerability to the impacts of climate change and because of international pressure [6]. Fossil  fuel  energy  consumption  must  be  reduced,  and  the  effects  of  CO 2 emissions  in Indonesia must be mitigated. Energy conservation (efficiency) and energy diversification are appropriate policy strategies. Accordingly, the Indonesian government should encourage decoupling [7], without weakening Indonesia's sustainable economic development.

Several  studies  have  used  the  decoupling  analysis  method.  Climent  and  Pardo  use decoupling  to  examine  the  correlation  between  gross  domestic  product  (GDP)  and energy  consumption  in  Spain  [8].  Diakoulaki  and  Mandaraka  adopted  it  to  evaluate progress  in  reducing  CO2 emissions  from  industrial  growth  in  14  European  Union countries [9]. Freitas and Kaneko used it to examine CO2  emissions and the growth of economic activity in Brazil [10]. Yang et al. used it to investigate whether there has been

Decoupling is a concept that links economic variables, energy variables, and environmental impacts [13-15]. The term decoupling is used to describe the relationship between economic activity and environmental degradation [10,15]. Specifically, decoupling refers to the relative growth rate of environmental stress and an easily connected economic variable. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) defines decoupling as the termination of the relationship between 'economic goods' and 'environmental crime'.  The  OECD  published  its  views  on  decoupling  as  an  indicator  of  sustainable development in 2002. There are  several  different  concept  definitions  of  decoupling  and measurement  methods.  At  present,  there  is  no  consensus  on  the  relative  strengths  and weaknesses of each [16]. The OECD developed the concept of measurement to distinguish between  absolute  decoupling  and  relative  decoupling  [17].  Absolute  decoupling  occurs when the variable growth of economic activity increases, but environmental variables show a stable trend or a downward trend over time. Meanwhile, relative decoupling occurs when the growth rate of environmental variables shows a positive trend but at a level lower than the growth of the variable economic activity (Figure 1).

Stern states that there are three component factors that can influence the decoupling of  environmental  impact  growth  (CO2 emissions)  and  economic activity  growth  [18], namely, (i) technological change, (ii) shifting the composition of energy inputs, and (iii) shifting composition the output. New discoveries and innovations (technological change) can lead to an increase in energy efficiency causing the rate of economic growth to be higher than the rate of growth of inputs or environmental impacts. The change from lowquality fuel to higher quality fuel will change the composition of energy input (change in energy share) and will reduce the energy intensity since less energy is needed to produce units of output. Changes in the composition of output (changes in share output) can also affect the relationship between energy and output (income) because different industries have different energy intensities. The decoupling factor component can contribute to the process of decoupling status when energy conservation (efficiency) and energy diversification (shift) policies are implemented effectively.

Energy efficiency (decreasing energy intensity) enables the industry  to reduce energy consumption in achieving certain output levels with lower costs and less pollution [19,20]. It can be considered as an additional energy source obtained from a reduction in energy consumption while maintaining output levels [19]. Thus, increasing the energy efficiency of the  industrial  sector  can  maintain  a  sustainable  energy  supply  [21].  Increased  energy efficiency  can  be  achieved  by  changing  technology  or  process  efficiency  [18,19].  CO 2 emissions  can  be  mitigated  by  substituting  environmentally  friendly  energy  or  shifting the composition of energy inputs [13,22,23]. The industrial sector can achieve the highest level of CO2 emission mitigation among all sectors with appropriate measures [21].

Globalisation demands that the manufacturing industry sector must apply standards focusing  on  the  raw  materials  efficiency,  energy  efficiency,  energy  diversification,  and low-carbon technology [24]. Thus, energy conservation policies, energy diversification, and minimisation of waste generation (CO2  emissions mitigation) should be applied by Indonesian manufacturing industry. Energy conservation (energy efficiency) and energy diversification (shifting structure of energy input composition) that are effective can meet the target of energy consumption and CO2  emissions in manufacturing industry; they thus  can  become  capital  for  the  future  [24].  The  target  to  be  achieved  is  total  CO 2 emissions decrease with growth in the industrial economy.

Indonesia's manufacturing industry developed significantly in the period 2010-2014. The GDP of industry grew around 28.04% with an average growth of 6.38% per year, despite diminishing indications (Table 1). In line with this growth, the final consumption of fossil fuel energy also grew, with an average of -2.17% per year (Table 1). Thus, the consumption of fossil fuel energy in manufacturing industry is not consistent with the direction of GDP growth. Besides, energy intensity shows a decline, which means there are already indications of energy efficiency in Indonesia's manufacturing industry. Does energy efficiency (decreasing energy intensity) indicate the existence of decoupling status in CO2 emissions? What components contribute to the existence of decoupling status in addition to the energy efficiency component?

This  study  aims  to  identify  the  existence  of  energy  efficiency  and  decoupling  CO 2 emissions in Indonesia's manufacturing industry. Accordingly, the aim is to: (1) estimate the CO2 emissions, (2) identify component effects that affect changes in CO2 emissions, and (3) identify the existence of decoupling CO2  emissions from industrial output.

## Data and method

The fundamental data used in this study concern energy consumption (based on fuel type) and the output of each firm in the period 2010-2014. The data are gathered from survey statistics of Indonesian large- and medium-sized industries prepared by Statistics Indonesia. Fuel energy consumed by Indonesian manufacturing industry firms is the type of  petrol,  diesel  oil,  kerosene,  coal,  natural  gas,  liquefied  petroleum  gas  (LPG),  and electricity.  Fuel  energy  consumption  data  are  available  in  different  physical  units.  To get  a  firm's  total  fuel  energy  consumption,  these  physical  values  are  standardised  to a  barrel  oil  equivalent  (BOE)  using  coefficients  issued  by  the  Ministry  of  Energy  and Mineral Resources and are then converted again to tonnes of coal equivalent (TCE) to adjust CO2 emission coefficient standards published by the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change [26]. Data on CO2  emissions can be estimated from fossil fuel energy consumption  data  based  on  the  Intergovernmental  Panel  on  Climate  Change  (IPCC) method. The output is measured as value added in billions of Indonesian rupiah based on 2010  constant  prices.  The  2010  figure  remains  valid  because  there  is  no  more  recent base year. This study covers the period 2010-2014 and so the value of output is based on 2010 constant prices.

In addition to data on energy consumption and output, the Statistics Indonesia data include other characteristics of firms, such as industrial sub-sector groups, intensity of technology used, and firm size. Industrial sub-sector groups are based on the International Standard Industrial Classification Rev. 4 (ISIC Rev.4). The firm is classified into 24 sub-sectors of industrial groups. Based on the classification of the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD), technological intensities of firms are classified into high (H), medium-high (M-H), medium (M), and medium-low (M-L) technologies. Firm size groups are based on the number of labourers. Small firms have less than 100 labourers, medium-sized firms have between 100 and 1000 labourers, and large firms have more than 1000 labourers.

## CO2  emissions decomposition

Decomposition  analysis  is  used  to  investigate  the  mechanisms  that  influence  energy consumption  and environmental side effects, especially  CO2 emissions [27-32]. Decomposition  analysis,  especially  index  decomposition  analysis  (IDA),  has  become widely used to track energy efficiency trends [33-35]. This technique studies the impact of  various  component  factors  that  contribute  to  changes  in  energy  consumption  and changes in CO2  emissions. This IDA technique is in the standardisation process [34]. This study uses IDA techniques, especially the Logarithmic Mean Divisia Index (LMDI) method [36-38]. The advantages of the LMDI method are that it decomposes completely, completes decomposition without residues [39,40], is consistent in aggregation [41], can be  applied  to  short  time  series,  and  is  easy  to  use  [42].  LMDI  is  the  most  preferred method in the context of IDA [43-45] because of the ease of its formulation. The method has a strong theoretical basis, is adaptable, and is readily interpreted [46].

IDA considers that five-component effects affect changes in CO2 emissions [36,44,45], as follows:

- (1) The  effect  of  industrial  economic  activity,  representing  the  total  output  of Indonesia's manufacturing industry.
- (2) The  effect  of  the  structure  of  the  industrial  economy,  representing  the  relative share  of  industrial  output  in  the  total  output  of  Indonesia's  manufacturing industry.
- (3) The  effect  of  energy  intensity,  representing  the  ratio  of  industrial  fossil  energy consumption and the value of industrial output.
- (4) The energy mix effect, representing the composition of fossil energy in the total energy consumption of Indonesia's manufacturing industry.

According to Diakoulaki and Mandaraka [9], there are several types of D T decoupling index values, as follows:

- (1) If D T ≥ 1, this indicates an absolute decoupling effect or 'strong decoupling efforts', which means the inhibiting effect of the four components on CO2  emissions is greater  than  the  effect  of  the  driving  component  of  the  growth  of  industrial economic activity, i.e. total CO2 emissions decrease while the industrial economy grows.

- (2) If 0 &lt; D T &lt; 1, this indicates a relative decoupling effect or 'weak decoupling effort', which means the reduction effect of the four components only compensates for a small part of CO2 emissions from the growth of industrial economic activity, i.e. total  CO2 emissions  grow  but  are  lower  than  growth  in  industrial  economic activity.
- (3) If D T &lt; 0, this indicates no decoupling or 'no decoupling effort,' which means the number of effects of the four components contributing to inhibiting CO2  emissions is not strong enough to reduce CO2  emissions significantly, i.e. CO2  emissions grow faster than growth output industrial economic activity.

If D T str , D T int , D T mix , and D T emf &gt; 0, it can be said that the effects of CO2 emission inhibiting components  such  as  industrial  economic  structure,  industrial  energy  intensity,  fuel energy consumption structure and enough substantive emission coefficients contribute to a partial decoupling effect. Conversely, if &lt; 0, these components do not contribute to decoupling [2,4].

## CO2 emissions decoupling status based on technology intensity

Table 4 presents the results of the analysis of CO2  emissions decoupling and industrial economic activity based on technology intensity. The total decoupling index is negative ( Dtot &lt; 0) for most technology intensity groups, showing that there is no decoupling effect between CO2  emissions and industrial economic activity. The reduction in CO2  emissions from the effect of the inhibiting component is less powerful than the increase in CO2 emissions  because  of  the  effect  of  the  component  of  the  growth  of  industrial economic  activity.  That  is,  as  industrial  economic  activity  increases,  CO 2 emissions also increase. Inhibiting components, namely, the structure of the industrial economy, industrial  energy  intensity  and  the  structure  of  the  energy  composition,  do  not  have a role in reducing CO2  emissions; instead, they contribute to increasing CO2  emissions together with increased industrial activity. These three components helped to increase CO2 emissions at medium-high (M-H) technology intensity (Table 2).

The decoupling index owing to the effect of the industrial economic structure is valued at 1.11 ( Dstr &gt; 1) and 0.3 (0 &lt; Dstr &lt; 1), respectively, at high (H) technology intensity and medium (M) technology intensity. Moreover, the decoupling index owing to the effect of industrial energy intensity is worth 0.26 (0 &lt; Dint &lt; 1) in medium-low (M-L) technology intensity. This shows that there are a partial absolute decoupling effect and a relatively partial decoupling effect, which means changes in the structure of the industrial economy or  changes  in  industrial  energy  intensity  make  a  very  significant  reduction  in  CO 2 emissions.  The  decoupling  index  of  industrial  economic  structure  is  partially  able  to show the existence of decoupling but is still weak because it has not been able to show the existence of total decoupling.

This  happens  in  the  high  (H)  technology  industry  and  medium  (M)  technology industry. Industrial economic structure decoupling index of high (H) and medium (M) technology intensity means that there is a shift in the industrial economic structure of the technology  intensity  from  high  energy-intensive  to  low  energy-intensive  or  there  is a  decrease  in  the  output  share  in  the  intensity  of  each  technology.  Thus,  the  partial decoupling effect of industrial economic structure on high (H) and medium (M) technology intensities occurs because the shared output of firms in the group has decreased in the  period  2010-2014.  Meanwhile,  the  decoupling  index  resulting  from  the  positive energy intensity effect means that there is a decrease in energy intensity or an increase in energy efficiency in the ML technology intensity, and the share of output has increased during the period 2010-2014.

The  decoupling  index  owing  to  the  effect  of  decreasing  energy  intensity  ( Dint )  in industries with M-L technology is positive, which is 0.26, but the decoupling index owing to changes in the structure of the industrial economy ( Dstr ) and changes in the structure

Conversely, in high (H) and medium (M) technologies, relatively partial decoupling occurs owing to the effects of industrial economic structure, but this effect is not able to cause the status of total decoupling because partial decoupling by the effects of energy intensity  and  the  effect  of  the  structure  of  the  energy  composition  does  not  occur. ## Conclusions and policy implications

## Conclusions

This study identifies the existence of decoupling CO2 emissions from economic activities in Indonesia's manufacturing industry according to three categories, namely, (1) industrial subsector, (2) technological intensity, and (3) firm size. Some important findings are as follows. First,  the  effect  of  energy  efficiency  (decrease  in  energy  intensity)  does  not  automatically imply the existence of total decoupling. In addition to energy intensity, total decoupling is also influenced by industrial structure effects and energy structure effects. Partial decoupling owing to the effect of energy intensity occurs in sub-sectors: heavy polluters (23), (10), (13), and (24); medium polluters (25), (14), and (15); and fewer polluters (28), (11), (31), and (33). The  total  decoupling  effect  occurs  in  sub-sectors:  absolute  decoupling  in  (15)  and  (33); relative decoupling in (13), (24), (25), (14), and (21); and no decoupling in (23), (10), (28), (11), and (31). Potential decoupling exists in the industrial sub-sectors (22), (17), (16), (29), (26), (30), (18), (32), and (19), and the increase of decoupling from relative decoupling to potential absolute decoupling in the industrial sub-sectors (24), (25), (14), and (21).

Second,  there  is  relative  decoupling  in  firms  with  medium-low  (M-L)  technology intensity but no decoupling in firms with other technology intensities. Assuming there is  no  change  in  the  structure  of  the  industrial  economy  and  an  increase  in  energy efficiency, there may be decoupling in firms with high technology intensity and medium technology intensity.

Third, by assuming there is no change in the structure of the industrial economy and an increase in energy efficiency, relative decoupling may occur in medium-sized firms

<!-- image -->

with 500-999 labourers and no decoupling in other firms. There may be decoupling in firms with 20-99 and 200-499 labourers.

## Policy implications

To strengthen a trend to decoupling, in line with a need for sustainable development, the Indonesian  government  should  both  focus  on  energy  efficiency  (energy  conservation) and also increase the shift in fuel composition (energy diversification) to good-quality fuels which will also improve environmental quality. The following policy implications can be proposed: (1) energy efficiency (reducing energy intensity), changing low-carbon technology or revitalising inefficient machines in sub-sectors, such as decoupling potential  in  sub-sectors  (22),  (17),  (29),  (16),  (26),  (30),  (18),  (32),  and  (19)  and  a  potential increase in the existence of decoupling from relative decoupling to absolute decoupling in sub-sectors  (24),  (14),  and  (21);  (2)  changing  low-carbon  technology  or  revitalising inefficient  machines  in  medium  or  high  technology  firms  and  firms  with  a  size 200-499 labourers; (3) providing subsidies or incentives to small firms that tend to be heavy polluters, so that they may instead use high-quality fuel (fewer emissions); and (4) promoting decoupling by increasing the tax/price of petroleum fuel (petrol) or reducing the tax/price of natural gas in Indonesia's manufacturing industry.
